# Swap the model, then verify everything

sqltext's best upgrades come in three layers. First, a better small model. Second, a set of cheap retrieval, repair and verification steps around it. Third, hard limits on what generated SQL can do; more sampling, bigger agents or a third-party gateway matter less. **Small models trained for SQL with SFT+RL (SLM-SQL, FINER-SQL, DatA-SQL) now report 51-67% BIRD dev execution accuracy at 0.5-1.5B parameters, against about 28% for the base Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B.** A 1.5B model quantised to about 3 bits should fit the 1 GB budget on paper, though nobody has measured its accuracy at that precision. On a CPU, the techniques that pay off are the ones that cost no LLM calls. **Value retrieval was worth about +2.5 to +4.8 BIRD points in three independent ablations.** Fixing wrong identifiers and literals, voting on execution results only when a confidence check flags doubt, and escalating to Claude are all cheaper than 30-64 local samples. For the analyzer, the research is blunt: **about 32% of LLM query rewrites return different results on real databases**, so "same rows on 5,000 rows and lower planner cost" is too weak a gate to accept a rewrite automatically. For safety, sqltext's SELECT-only parser blocks write attacks. It does not block reads of rows the user should not see, side-effecting functions called from SELECT, runaway queries, or instructions planted in table rows that reach Claude through the RLM agent. A sixteen-question eval cannot detect any of these regressions. The prioritised list at the end puts safety hardening, Bedrock reliability and value retrieval first, because they are cheap and well supported. Model swaps, the analyzer verification cascade and RLM changes follow, gated on a larger eval set.

## A SQL-tuned 1.5B model at ~3 bits is the biggest single lever, but its accuracy is unmeasured

The small-model field changed in 2025-2026. Generalist instruct models are now well behind small models trained on SQL with chain-of-thought (CoT) data and then GRPO reinforcement learning. **SLM-SQL reports 56.87 BIRD dev EX on Qwen2.5-Coder-0.5B, 59.52 on Qwen3-0.6B, 62.19 on deepseek-coder-1.3B and 67.08 on Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B.** It also reports 73.50 and 79.06 on Spider *test* for the 0.5B and 1.5B models without any Spider-specific training ([SLM-SQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.22478)). **FINER-SQL reports 50.85 / 70.2 (BIRD dev / Spider dev) at 0.5B and 63.17 / 80.0 at 1.5B** ([FINER-SQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.03465)). **DatA-SQL-1.5B reports 55.86 BIRD dev** at Vote@8 ([model card](https://huggingface.co/Chinastark/DatA-SQL-1.5B)). The older baselines trail: Prem-1B-SQL at 46.0 BIRD dev ([model card](https://huggingface.co/prem-research/prem-1B-SQL)), CodeS-1B at 50.46 BIRD / 77.9 Spider as cited by FINER-SQL, and untrained Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B-Instruct at 28.40 ([SLM-SQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.22478)). No OmniSQL, XiYan or Arctic-Text2SQL checkpoint is smaller than 3B. The strongest small text-to-SQL families therefore all derive from Qwen2.5-Coder, the same base sqltext already ships.

The catch is how the headline numbers were produced. **SLM-SQL samples 64 SQL candidates plus 8 merge-revision passes per question. FINER-SQL samples 30 candidates at T=1.0 and takes a majority vote** ([SLM-SQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.22478); [FINER-SQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.03465)). Neither paper publishes a single-sample greedy figure. SLM-SQL's ablation shows that removing its corrective self-consistency step costs about 5 points. One greedy pass is therefore probably several points below the headline (an inference from the ablation). The larger gains come from training: removing SFT costs 21.93 points at 0.5B. FINER-SQL's README hints at where the remaining headroom is. Its 0.5B model's majority vote reaches 50.85, but the correct SQL appears somewhere among the 30 candidates **68.32%** of the time ([FINER-SQL GitHub](https://github.com/thanhdath/finer-sql)). Choosing the right candidate is the bottleneck, not producing more of them.

Memory is tight but workable. The size gap between quantisers comes from how they store the embedding tensor, not from the model. **Switching sqltext's current model from the official 491 MB Q4_K_M to bartowski's 398 MB file saves about 90 MB at no expected accuracy cost** (inference from identical weights). A Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B-family model has 28 layers and 2 KV heads, so its f16 KV cache is roughly 117 MB at `n_ctx=4096` (computed from the [config](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B-Instruct/blob/main/config.json)). With an IQ3_XXS file (669 MB) and about 150 MB of runtime overhead, the total lands near **0.94 GB**. That figure is an estimate, not a measurement. There is a second problem: sqltext's own guard in `sqltext/hardware.py` (`file_GiB × 1.25 + 0.3` against a 1.0 GB default) works out to about 1.08 GB for that file and would refuse it. The heuristic needs replacing with a measured file + KV + overhead model before any 1.5B candidate can load.

The evidence on accuracy loss is thin. **I found no published measurement of GGUF Q3/IQ3/IQ2 loss on Spider or BIRD for any model at 1.5B or below.** The closest study, run at 4-bit on Qwen2.5-Instruct, found that **"moving to a larger model size typically results in higher accuracy"** than spending 8× inference compute on a smaller one. In that study 8× compute closed only 50-76% of the gap to the next size ([arXiv 2608.25761](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.25761)). That weakly favours "1.5B at ~3 bits" over "0.5B at 4 bits", but sqltext has to measure it, and IQ3 should be treated as the floor (inference). Licensing narrows the field further. Most SLM-SQL checkpoints are non-commercial, and SLM-SQL-1.5B's apache-2.0 tag sits on an almost empty card. That leaves **DatA-SQL-1.5B as the cleanest strong candidate with a small ready-made imatrix quant**, and SLM-SQL-1.5B as the stronger option if its license is confirmed and an i1 quant is built locally with `llama-quantize --imatrix`. Every RL-tuned model also emits `<think>…</think>` and, for SLM-SQL, `<answer>…</answer>`. `sqltext/prompt.py` and `sqltext/backends/llama.py` would need each model's template, an answer extractor, and a `max_tokens` cap. Think tokens are a real cost on a CPU, and I found no CPU throughput figures for any of these fine-tunes.

## Retrieval and repair beat sampling when every forward pass is on a CPU

The most consistent result in the technique literature costs no LLM calls. **Value retrieval, meaning matching question phrases to actual database values, is worth −4.76 (CHESS), −2.92 (CHASE-SQL) and −3.19 for CodeS-1B when removed on BIRD dev** ([CHESS](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.16755); [CHASE-SQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01943); [CodeS](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.16347)). On Spider it barely matters (−0.2 TS for CodeS), because Spider questions mostly quote their literals verbatim. CHESS builds an offline MinHash-LSH index over all distinct values, which cut lookup from "5 minutes to 5 seconds". CodeS pairs BM25 with longest-common-substring re-ranking. OmniSQL, the strongest open text-to-SQL family, trains its models to expect "question-relevant values" as per-column comments in the DDL ([OmniSQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.02240)). sqltext's three sampled values per text column show the model what values look like, but rarely surface the value a question refers to. A question-conditioned retriever built on SQLite FTS5 trigram, BM25 or `datasketch` LSH, with `rapidfuzz` re-ranking, would fix that. It would also make the literal grounding check in `sqltext/verify.py` smarter: a SQL literal that equals a retrieved DB value (for example "NY" → 'New York') should count as grounded rather than trigger an escalation (inference).

Schema linking is a recall problem at this scale. Linking ablations show +2 to +6 points: CHESS loses 6.12 without table selection, and MCS-SQL gains 2.1 ([MCS-SQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.07467)). "The Death of Schema Linking?" shows that strong long-context models do well with the full schema, and that imperfect linking "can often exclude required columns" ([arXiv 2408.07702](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.07702)). The two results fit together. A 0.5-1.5B model is easily distracted, so filtering helps it, but the filter must favour recall. The escalation model should receive the full or lightly filtered schema so that a local filter miss does not propagate. XiYan's M-Schema format (types, PK markers, example values) beat plain DDL by **+2.03** on average across four LLMs ([XiYan-SQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.08599)). For embeddings on a 1 GB budget, model2vec's static `potion-base-8M` is about 8 MB and depends only on numpy ([model2vec](https://github.com/MinishLab/model2vec)). No published BIRD/Spider ablation exists for it, so recommending it rests on size and speed, not text-to-SQL evidence.

Candidate selection helps, but compute makes it a gated tool rather than a default. OmniSQL's 8-sample execution vote lifted base Qwen2.5-Coder-7B from **50.9 to 61.3** on BIRD dev, yet added only about 2 points for its own fine-tuned models, and slightly hurt OmniSQL-7B on Spider ([OmniSQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.02240)). At sqltext's scale, the 2026 grammar-constrained study measured Spider dev EX rising from **0.139 to 0.245 at 0.5B and 0.351 to 0.505 at 1.5B** with beam budget 1 → 8. Beam beat sample-and-vote at equal budget in 11 of 16 configurations ([arXiv 2608.25761](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.25761)). CHASE-SQL shows the ceiling: its oracle across all candidates scored 82.79 against 73.01 for its trained selector, again making selection the bottleneck ([CHASE-SQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01943)). The practical design for sqltext is a confidence-gated vote (inference). Generate greedily. Only when a cheap signal flags doubt (a parse or execution error, a grounding failure, an empty result, or low mean token logprob) draw 3-5 more samples, execute them with a row cap and timeout, and cluster by normalised result set. Low agreement then becomes an escalation trigger instead of a reason to sample more.

Constrained decoding needs care. PICARD's incremental-parse beam search added 5-10 EX points to fine-tuned T5 on Spider ([PICARD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.05093)). For modern instruction-tuned models, full SQL grammars are often incomplete and backfire. Generic grammar-constrained decoding dropped Llama-70B on BIRD from 56.6 to 36.4. **TTD-SQL's lighter approach, which constrains only table and column identifiers via prefix tries, matched unconstrained accuracy, raised CodeLlama-7B's executable rate on BIRD from 57.6 to 67.8, and sped up decoding by up to about 20%** ([TTD-SQL](https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-industry.90.pdf)). The 2026 small-model study found a schema-aware identifier grammar slightly *helped* at 0.5B and 1.5B. It hurt at 3B and 7B because it could not express `IN` lists, `IS NOT NULL` or outer joins ([arXiv 2608.25761](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.25761)). llama-cpp-python exposes `grammar`, `logits_processor` and `logprobs` ([API reference](https://llama-cpp-python.readthedocs.io/en/latest/api-reference/)). An identifier-only constraint in `sqltext/backends/llama.py` is therefore feasible. Its CPU overhead is unmeasured, and post-hoc sqlglot identifier repair (fuzzy-map an unknown column to the nearest schema name when the match is unambiguous) covers much of the same failure with no decoding change.

Two other techniques split by model size. Prompted CoT hurts small untrained models: zero-shot "think step by step" cut CodeLlama-7B on BIRD from **23.2 to 12.0** ([TTD-SQL](https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-industry.90.pdf)). CoT is a gain only when the model was *trained* to reason, as with SLM-SQL. Few-shot examples were MCS-SQL's largest single ablation component (+4.8 on BIRD dev). DAIL-SQL's masked-question selection beat random selection by about 3 points for GPT-4 on Spider, but DAIL also observed fine-tuned models getting *worse* with in-context examples ([DAIL-SQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.15363)). A per-database store of verified question-SQL pairs is a natural source of examples and of exact-match cache hits. Whether it helps a given fine-tuned local model has to be tested before it is turned on. Execution-feedback fixers are well supported for larger models (CHASE's query fixer −3.78 when removed; CHESS's revision tool −6.80). DIN-SQL found that weaker models need concrete correction prompts such as "column X does not exist; available: A, B, C" rather than generic "check for bugs" prompts ([DIN-SQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.11015)). Rule-based fixes should run before any LLM retry, and an empty result should count as a soft error.

## Execution signals, not an off-the-shelf router, should decide when to call Claude

Learned routers report large savings. RouteLLM reports more than 2× cost reduction, FrugalGPT up to 98% at GPT-4 quality, Hybrid LLM 40% fewer large-model calls, and AutoMix more than 50% ([RouteLLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18665); [FrugalGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05176); [Hybrid LLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14618v1); [AutoMix](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.12963)). All were trained on general chat and QA preference data, and none has been evaluated on BIRD or Spider. RouteLLM's recommended `mf` router also needs an OpenAI key to compute embeddings, so question text leaves the machine ([RouteLLM GitHub](https://github.com/lm-sys/RouteLLM)). sqltext has something these routers lack: it can run the SQL. The direct evidence on confidence for text-to-SQL points that way. **Log-probability and plain self-consistency predict correctness at only 0.61-0.68 AUROC. An LLM judge reaches 0.72-0.78, and a two-provider judge ensemble 0.82** ([arXiv 2607.06799](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.06799), a single-author preprint). **An AST-node classifier built on schema validity, alias resolution and type compatibility improved AUC by 27.44% over token log-probabilities and held up across databases** ([arXiv 2511.13984](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.13984)). Execution signals add information on top of both ([arXiv 2508.14056](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.14056)). The right router for `sqltext/gateway.py` is AutoMix-shaped: answer locally, verify structurally and by execution, then escalate. The rule-based `classify()` stays as a prior. Every routing outcome gets logged, and a small logistic model fitted on sqltext's own features is calibrated to a target escalation rate, borrowing RouteLLM's `calibrate_threshold` idea (inference). All the AUROC studies used models larger than 0.5B, so these signals need re-measuring on sqltext's own evals.

A gateway product would add more risk than value. **Bedrock Intelligent Prompt Routing supports only Claude 3 Haiku, 3.5 Haiku and the 3.5 Sonnet versions, is "only optimized for English prompts", and cannot use application-specific performance data** ([Bedrock prompt routing](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/prompt-routing.html)). It cannot route to Haiku 4.5, Opus 5.5 or a local model. LiteLLM's Router has the right features: fallbacks, cooldowns, budgets and caching ([LiteLLM routing](https://docs.litellm.ai/docs/routing)). But on 24 March 2026, **LiteLLM 1.82.7 and 1.82.8 shipped a credential-stealing payload that harvested SSH keys and cloud credentials** ([NetSPI](https://www.netspi.com/blog/executive-blog/ai-ml-pentesting/litellm-supply-chain-compromise/)). That is a serious exposure for a tool that holds AWS credentials. The better move is to copy the patterns in about 150 lines.

The current `sqltext/backends/bedrock.py` leaves several of those patterns unused. It sets no timeout or `max_retries`, so the SDK defaults apply: a **10-minute timeout and 2 retries**, which can compound to `timeout × 3` of wall-clock time (Anthropic SDK reference, [anthropic-sdk-python](https://github.com/anthropics/anthropic-sdk-python)). It also sends `system` as a plain string with no `cache_control`. Prompt caching on Bedrock has a model-specific floor: **Opus 5.5 caches from 512 tokens, but Haiku 4.5 needs at least 4,096 tokens before a checkpoint, and a checkpoint below the minimum "succeeds" silently without caching** ([Bedrock prompt caching](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/prompt-caching.html)). Cache hits do not count against Bedrock rate limits, so caching also eases throttling. The RLM root resends the schema on every turn and always thinks, which makes it the clear winner. Haiku escalations benefit only on medium and large schemas. Cross-Region inference profiles absorb throttling at no extra charge, and **global profiles are about 10% cheaper** but give up data residency ([cross-Region inference](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/cross-region-inference.html)). The notes did not confirm whether the Mantle endpoint accepts `us.`/`global.` profile IDs. At first-party list prices, **Opus 5.5 costs $4/$20 per million input/output tokens against $1/$5 for Haiku 4.5** ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing.md)). Bedrock prices are partner-set and were not verified. Anthropic's own guidance is to measure the capable model at lower `effort` before adding cascade tiers, because each extra model splits the prompt cache. That argues against adding more Bedrock tiers until an eval shows they pay.

## The RLM agent should recurse less, remember more and compress harder

The Recursive Language Model paper holds the long input in a REPL variable and lets the root model call `llm_query` over slices. **Only truncated stdout metadata ever enters the root's history.** Its main results all use **depth 1**, with a GPT-5 root and GPT-5-mini sub-calls, at an average cost of $0.11-$0.99 per query ([arXiv 2512.24601](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.24601)). An independent 2026 reproduction is the key warning for sqltext's depth-2 setting. **Depth 2 degraded results everywhere: Kimi K2 fell from 86.6% to 55.0% on OOLONG, and DeepSeek's S-NIAH latency rose from 89.3 s at depth 1 to 344.5 s at depth 2.** The authors blamed format collapse and "excessive verification loops" ([arXiv 2603.02615](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.02615v1)). No published work applies RLMs to text-to-SQL itself. The transferable lessons are architectural (inference). Default `sqltext/rlm.py` to depth 1 and allow depth 2 only when an explicit decomposition heuristic fires. Keep bulk `describe_tables` and `run_probe` output out of the root's context, behind handles. Treat `submit_sql` as the only terminal action, mirroring the paper's brittle `FINAL()` tags, and reject submissions that are prose or a plan.

The best documented large-schema systems already use something close to sqltext's tool set. **AutoLink's four actions are `explore_schema`, `retrieve_schema`, `verify_schema` and `add_schema`.** They correspond to `run_probe`, `search_schema` and part of `submit_sql`, with no sqltext equivalent for `add_schema`. AutoLink keeps **about 90% strict recall on schemas above 3,000 columns, where baselines fall below 40%**. It uses 21.2K tokens per Spider 2.0-Lite example against 81.1K for ReFoRCE, and its gains level off after 4-6 of its 10 allowed turns ([AutoLink](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.17190v1)). It seeds exploration with a column-level embedding index (name, table, type, description) and a generous top-50 to top-100. ReFoRCE adds three ideas. It groups sharded tables by name pattern, which shrank GA360's DDL from over 50 MB to under 2 MB; removing that compression costs about 3.5 EX. It votes over 8 candidates and treats only a unique maximum as high confidence. It runs column exploration (at most 10 probes, LIMIT 20) only on the roughly 100 of 547 tied cases ([ReFoRCE](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.00675)). Production systems add curated context. Snowflake Cortex Analyst retrieves verified queries and fuzzy-matched literals, and rejects ambiguous questions upfront instead of guessing ([Cortex Analyst](https://snowflake.com/engineering-blog/snowflake-cortex-analyst-behind-the-scenes)). The AT&T/RelationalAI "Relational Knowledge Graph" entry scores 86.28 on Spider 2.0-Snow ([leaderboard](https://spider2-sql.github.io/)).

For sqltext this points to five changes (inference). Upgrade `search_schema` from keyword to hybrid search (BM25 plus column embeddings plus a value index). Collapse table families in `list_tables`. Add a working-set tool and a `find_join_path` tool over the FK graph. Split the single 16-step budget into phase budgets: discovery ≤6 steps, ≤10 probes, ≤3 sub-questions, ≤3-5 refinements, with `submit_sql` forced when 2 steps remain. Return structured tool errors with nearest-name suggestions. Leaderboard numbers deserve caution here. **ReFoRCE + o3 is listed at 62.89 on Spider 2.0-Snow but reported 35.83 in its own paper. AutoLink is listed at 52.28 on Lite against 34.92 in its paper.** The leaderboard says scores change as metrics are re-checked ([leaderboard](https://spider2-sql.github.io/)). The cause of the gap could not be confirmed.

## Matching rows on 5,000 sample rows is the analyzer's weakest gate

The strongest finding in the optimisation research directly targets `sqltext/analyzer.py`. Microsoft's CIDR 2026 study rewrote 118 real enterprise queries and 224 benchmark queries with LLMs. **About 32% of the rewrites produced different results on the given database**, and matching results on a database "is only a necessary condition but not sufficient for equivalence" ([CIDR 2026](https://www.vldb.org/cidrdb/papers/2026/p33-narasayya.pdf)). The median improvement of the best verified rewrite was only ≤6%, but the P90 was 63-83%. Rewriting pays off on a few queries, and verification has to filter out most of the rest. Every serious free-form rewriter wraps generation in a cascade. GenRewrite tries SQLSolver first, then the SlabCity counterexample tester, and **abstains if neither certifies** ([GenRewrite](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.09060)). LITHE combines correlated sampling, injected boundary tuples, QED/SQLSolver, and full-data comparison. It also requires a **>1.5× estimated cost speedup** because optimiser cost and runtime diverge ([LITHE](https://openproceedings.org/2026/conf/edbt/paper-93.pdf)). Rule-selection systems avoid the problem by construction. R-Bot picks Calcite rules and cut a bank's slow-query workload from 9.23 h to 4.37 h ([R-Bot](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01661)). sqltext already ships sqlglot, whose optimiser passes (`unnest_subqueries`, `eliminate_joins`, `merge_subqueries`, `simplify`) provide a deterministic rewrite track. Canonicalising both queries with them gives a fast, heuristic syntactic-equivalence check, not a proof.

Formal checkers exist but cover a minority of realistic rewrites. **SQLSolver verified 18.9% of GenRewrite's TPC-DS rewrites and 74.4% on JOB** ([GenRewrite](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.09060)). LITHE could formally verify only 11 of 27 productive rewrites. Microsoft reports that QED verified none of its real-world LLM rewrites ([CIDR 2026](https://www.vldb.org/cidrdb/papers/2026/p33-narasayya.pdf)). The usable tools differ in form. SQLSolver is a Java 17 jar using Calcite syntax that returns EQ/NEQ/UNKNOWN ([SQLSolver](https://github.com/SJTU-IPADS/SQLSolver)). QED is Rust and cannot handle ORDER BY/LIMIT ([QED](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol17/p3602-wang.pdf)). **VeriEQL is Python + Z3, proves or disproves bounded equivalence with counterexample databases, handles ORDER BY, CASE and outer joins, and solved 77% of 24,455 benchmarks** ([VeriEQL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03193)); its license was not confirmed. The right output is a two-axis label rather than accept/reject (inference). Equivalence would be PROVEN / BOUNDED / TESTED / UNVERIFIED, and performance MEASURED / ESTIMATED / PLAN-SHAPE. Only PROVEN plus a margin would be auto-accepted. Two cheap fixes should land first: compare result sets as multisets, and treat `LIMIT` without a total `ORDER BY` as non-deterministic. SQLFluff's AM09 rule flags that pattern ([SQLFluff rules](https://docs.sqlfluff.com/en/stable/reference/rules.html)), and today it can make the "same rows" check pass or fail by chance.

The index advisor can move from single-column guesses to what-if-verified composite suggestions on three of four engines. **HypoPG creates session-private hypothetical indexes that plain EXPLAIN uses, and reports their estimated size** ([HypoPG](https://hypopg.readthedocs.io/en/rel1_stable/usage.html)). Dexter (MIT) and Supabase's `index_advisor` (which returns before/after costs and DDL) build on it ([Dexter](https://github.com/ankane/dexter); [index_advisor](https://github.com/supabase/index_advisor)). **SQL Server's `SET SHOWPLAN_XML ON` returns the estimated plan without executing, including `MissingIndexes` with an estimated impact percentage** ([SHOWPLAN_XML](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/sql/t-sql/statements/set-showplan-xml-transact-sql); [missing-index guidance](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/sql/relational-databases/indexes/tune-nonclustered-missing-index-suggestions)). That one change closes sqltext's "no EXPLAIN for SQL Server" gap and adds an engine-native advisor. SQLite's experimental `.expert` proposes indexes ([SQLite CLI](https://sqlite.org/cli.html)). MySQL/MariaDB offer no hypothetical index in the docs consulted; invisible or ignored indexes only test *removing* one ([MySQL invisible indexes](https://dev.mysql.com/doc/refman/8.4/en/invisible-indexes.html)). Postgres DDL should use `CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY` with lock and statement timeouts, following squawk's migration-safety rules ([squawk](https://squawkhq.com/docs/rules)). For plan reading, **PostgreSQL warns that `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` actually executes the statement**, that per-node rows and times must be multiplied by `loops`, and that toy-sized tables produce plans that do not extrapolate ([PG EXPLAIN](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/sql-explain.html); [Using EXPLAIN](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/using-explain.html)). An opt-in analyze mode should therefore run in a rolled-back, read-only, time-limited transaction. Once it has actual rows, mechanical checks are straightforward: estimated-versus-actual row misestimates, hash batches above 1, disk sorts, and high rows-removed-by-filter ratios. The 10×/100× misestimate thresholds are an engineering choice with no published source.

## A SELECT-only parser stops writes, not reads, sleeps or poisoned rows

The threat model is well documented. Pedro et al. (ICSE 2025) showed prompt-to-SQL injection working against **all seven LLMs tested and nearly all real LangChain/LlamaIndex apps**. They found that a parser-level SELECT filter is a complete defence only against write bypass (RD.1) and the injected multi-step attack (RI.2). It does not stop reads of other users' rows (RD.2) or answer manipulation by a poisoned row (RI.1) ([ICSE'25](https://syssec.dpss.inesc-id.pt/papers/pedro_icse25.pdf)). Their 2026 follow-up adds that indirect attacks "slipped through" even single-statement limits. They advise that "even data coming from 'trusted' internal sources must be sanitized before being fed back to the model". They also cite CVEs where "nominally read-only SQL tooling assumptions were bypassed" ([IEEE S&P](https://syssec.dpss.inesc-id.pt/papers/pedro_ieeesp26.pdf)). `sqltext/safety.py` implements exactly that parser layer: one `exp.Query`, no write nodes, no `INTO`. sqltext's RLM agent feeds `run_probe` results to Claude, which is the RI.1/RI.2 channel. Their LLM-guard layer, which inspects results before the main model sees them, reported **99.55% detection over 1,120 malicious prompts at 0.43 s per check**.

What the parser lets through is often legal SELECT syntax. In PostgreSQL 18, **advisory-lock functions carry no privilege restriction, `set_config` changes session parameters, and `pg_read_file`-class functions bypass in-database privilege checks once granted** ([PG admin functions](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/functions-admin.html)). A PG `READ ONLY` transaction "does not prevent all writes to disk" and allows temp-table writes ([SET TRANSACTION](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/sql-set-transaction.html)). MySQL's `LOAD_FILE` and `SELECT … INTO OUTFILE` depend on `secure_file_priv` ([MySQL system variables](https://dev.mysql.com/doc/refman/8.4/en/server-system-variables.html)). `MAX_EXECUTION_TIME` covers only top-level read-only SELECTs ([MySQL optimizer hints](https://dev.mysql.com/doc/refman/en/optimizer-hints.html)). For SQLite, Python offers `mode=ro` URIs, `set_authorizer` (deny ATTACH and PRAGMA, or NULL-mask a column), `set_progress_handler` as a timeout, and `setlimit` ([Python sqlite3](https://docs.python.org/3/library/sqlite3.html)). OWASP's guidance for excessive agency is to enforce authorisation in the downstream system, not the LLM, and to run with the user's own least privileges ([OWASP LLM06](https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm062025-excessive-agency/)). sqltext's defence should therefore have four layers (inference). The first is a per-dialect allow-list of `exp.Func`/`exp.Anonymous` names, with unknown or user-defined functions denied by default. The second is server-side timeouts on every engine. The third is DB-enforced read-only on MySQL and SQLite, as well as Postgres. The fourth is documented least-privilege roles, since a superuser DSN defeats several layers at once. Function-level examples beyond the cited PG docs (MySQL `SLEEP`/`BENCHMARK`/`GET_LOCK`, the dblink loopback bypass) come from training knowledge in the notes and should be confirmed by tests in the repo.

Data flow to Bedrock is the other open front. Snowflake's conservative reference design sends only semantic-model metadata to the LLM and executes under platform RBAC ([Cortex Analyst docs](https://docs.snowflake.com/en/user-guide/snowflake-cortex/cortex-analyst)). sqltext's agent sends sample values and probe rows. It should classify PII-like columns by name and type, mask sample values, offer a per-connection consent flag for sending values, wrap probe results in delimited "data, not instructions" blocks with per-cell truncation, and cap total probes per question (inference, following LangShield's layers).

Evaluation is the precondition for trusting any of these changes. Single-database execution match produces false positives, and test-suite accuracy exists because the earlier Spider metric showed a **2.5% average and 8.1% worst-case false-negative rate** ([Zhong et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.02840)). Public labels are noisy too: **52.8% of BIRD Mini-Dev and 62.8% of Spider 2.0-Snow items carry annotation errors, and correcting them shifted 16 agents' ranks by up to ±9** ([arXiv 2601.08778](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.08778v1)). Robustness slices show large drops: on Dr.Spider the most robust model lost 14.0% overall and 50.7% on the hardest perturbation ([Dr.Spider](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.08881)). In `examples/eval_demo.py`, sixteen questions put one question at 6.25 points, so most of the gains in this report (2-5 points) are invisible to the current eval. BIRD Mini-Dev's 500 examples cover SQLite, MySQL and PostgreSQL, three of sqltext's four dialects ([BIRD](https://bird-bench.github.io/)). That makes it the natural public slice, provided failures are hand-audited before being trusted. A separate security suite should cover direct and indirect P2SQL, dangerous functions, DoS queries and PII canaries.

## Numbers that conflict or remain unverified

Several figures above should not be quoted without checking the primary source. The SLM-SQL paper's table, as extracted, gives 70.60-72.08 (0.5B) and 75.15-76.72 (1.5B). Those conflict with the abstract's 56.87 and 67.08 and look implausibly high for BIRD dev; the abstract numbers are used here ([SLM-SQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.22478)). FINER-SQL-0.5B's Spider dev score is 70.2 in the paper but 75.0 in the README, probably because the README uses a Spider-specific checkpoint. The 1.5B FINER checkpoint is not published on Hugging Face ([FINER-SQL GitHub](https://github.com/thanhdath/finer-sql)). Prem-1B-SQL's license is listed as both apache-2.0 and MIT, and its GGUF sizes match deepseek-coder-1.3B byte for byte, which suggests it is really a ~1.3B model ([model card](https://huggingface.co/prem-research/prem-1B-SQL)). Spider 2.0 leaderboard entries for ReFoRCE and AutoLink run 17-27 points above their own papers. BIRD's test leader differs by source: GrainSQL 82.95% on the BIRD site versus AskData 81.95% in a secondary aggregator ([BIRD](https://bird-bench.github.io/)). The RLM per-benchmark table came from a summarised fetch and needs checking against the PDF. The 0.94 GB RAM figure for a 1.5B IQ3_XXS model is arithmetic, not a measurement. No source measured GGUF 3-bit accuracy loss on text-to-SQL, CPU tokens per second for any SQL fine-tune, Bedrock-side prices or quotas for Haiku 4.5 and Opus 5.5, or whether Mantle accepts cross-Region profile IDs. The 99.55% LLM-guard detection rate is the authors' own evaluation, and the confidence-signal AUROCs come from a single-author preprint.

## Conclusion

The evidence moves where sqltext should spend effort. The local model is no longer the obvious weak link to escalate around. A SQL-tuned 1.5B model at 3 bits has a credible chance of beating today's 0.5B by double digits within the same memory, so the RAM heuristic and the eval set now stand between sqltext and its largest accuracy gain. Several published methods converge on one point: selection and verification, not generation, are the bottleneck. FINER-SQL produces a correct candidate 68% of the time but its vote picks one only 51% of the time. CHASE-SQL's selector trails its own oracle by about 10 points. A third of LLM rewrites fail on real data. Value retrieval, AST-level checks, execution agreement and tiered equivalence verification are the same capability applied to generation, routing and optimisation, so building them once in `verify.py` pays off across sqltext.

The security finding changes what "read-only" should mean for sqltext. Read-only has to be enforced by the database, with timeouts and least-privilege roles, and RLM probe results have to be treated as untrusted input to Claude. The parser stays as the first filter, not the boundary. A smaller tool that verifies everything it runs, and labels clearly what it could not verify, will be more robust than a larger cascade that trusts its own outputs.
